\section{Introduction} 
\label{sec:intro}

On June 29, 2026, the Supreme Court of the United States released two different rulings regarding executive power. In \textcite[slip op. at 2, 5]{trump_slaughter_2026}, the Supreme Court argued that the US President has the power to fire heads of Congressionally-established independent agencies 'at will" and without "cause." On the same day, the Supreme Court released Trump v. Cook, regarding the Federal Reserve (Fed). Since the start of President Trump's second term, the President has repeatedly attempted to influence the Fed to lower interest rates, using a variety of threats and political pressure. These efforts culminated in President Trump attempting to fire Governor Lisa Cook based on claims of mortgage fraud. At the time of writing this paper, these mortgage fraud claims had not been proven in court, and Governor Cook had not been given the opportunity to respond to these claims in a legal setting.

In Trump v. Cook, the Supreme Court ruled that the President could not remove Governor Cook without proving that she had committed mortgage fraud in a legal setting. While the President can remove policymakers at the Fed with sufficient ``cause," he had not demonstrated such cause regarding Governor Cook. This notably differs from the sweeping executive powers established in Trump v. Slaughter to fire heads of other agencies without cause. Thus, the Supreme Court appeared to carve out a constitutional protection for the Federal Reserve, legally distinguishing it from other agencies. To justify this, the court made references to the history of central banking in the United States. However, it also referenced arguments that are echoed in some economics literature. For instance, the court argues that ``it is the Federal Reserve’s independence that allows it to pursue its mandate of 'maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates'" \parencite[slip op. at 14]{trump_cook_2026}. This leads to an interesting question: what are the underlying academic arguments that justify this position?

Central bank independence has been extensively written about in academic settings for decades. Some of the most well-cited academic papers in the world, written by economists, describe the merits of the current institutional arrangement of America's central bank. They cite the time-inconsistency problem and core macroeconomic theory to argue that monetary policy can only be effective if it is removed from the political process. Others, predominantly political scientists and public policy academics, question this rhetoric. Some criticize central bank independence as antidemocratic, while others suggest independent central banks create policies that benefit financial institutions and enhance inequality. Through this debate, the two sets of literature rarely directly engage with each other. The economics papers generally ignore criticisms from the political science world, and vice versa. In the economics arena, independence is settled fact; in the political science arena, it is dangerous and problematic.


This paper asks two core questions. First, what normative assumptions underpin the economics literature that supports central bank independence? Second, do these assumptions influence the way policymakers at the Federal Reserve communicate about monetary policy? To do this, I conduct a methodological analysis of some of the most famous economics papers on independence to discover where these papers made political assumptions that influenced their ultimate findings. In particular, I investigate the underlying modeling assumptions that are used in both theoretical and empirical analysis on central bank independence. I do not seek to justify the Supreme Court's rulings on executive power. Instead, I seek to understand the logic of what Federal Reserve independence is and why it is important, as this logic may help justify why the Federal Reserve is ``different" relative to other executive agencies.

%Add a quick definition up here of instrument and goal independence?
My core finding is that the economics literature regularly conflates instrument and goal independence, and this significantly influences political and economic discourse on independence. First, the literature distinguishes between instrument independence, where the Federal Reserve is able to use its monetary policy tools to achieve its legal objectives, and goal independence, where the Federal Reserve has the flexibility to choose the goals of monetary policy. Theoretical models that defend independence  the current institutional arrangement argue that the goals of monetary policy should match those of the ``conservative central bank" who is more inflation-averse than the general public. In practice, this means that the goals of monetary policy are presupposed by the literature, thereby closing the epistemic debate on what the optimal goals of monetary policy should be. I then trace this epistemic process into Federal Reserve speeches, where the conflation is often addressed as a methodological critique of the literature, yet the overall findings of the literature are nonetheless supported and defended. In economics models, the ``conservative central banker" is a banker who matches the interests of the financial sector regarding inflation. Thus, the epistemically closed debate on the goals of monetary policy encodes those interests into baseline academic research.

Section \ref{sec:background_all} provides a baseline understanding of the economics and political science literature (and their differing conclusions on central bank independence). Section \ref{sec:methodology} handles methodology. Section \ref{sec:analysis_all} provides the economics literature analysis. The theoretical economics literature that developed the conservative central banker model is analyzed if \ref{sec:analysis_theoretical_models}, while \ref{sec:analysis_empiricial_cbi_indices} describes empirical analysis and measures of independence, focusing on ``de jure," or legal independence. \ref{sec:gov_speeches_analysis} studies how the literature is utilized in the policymaking setting through Federal Reserve officials speeches. Section \ref{sec:conclusion} provides conclusions and discussions.


\section{Background and Theory}
\label{sec:background_all}

\subsection{Economics Central Bank Independence Literature}
\label{sec:background_economics}

I begin by providing a more precise definition of central bank independence (CBI). \textcite{debelle_how_1994} distinguishes two core types of CBI: goal and instrument independence. Instrument independence handles the actual implementation of monetary policy to achieve those goals. When a central bank has instrument independence, is it able to utilize the tools of monetary policy to best achieve preset goals independently from political influence. In such a system, a political agent, such as the legislature or a president, is unable to influence the decision-making process for choosing which tools to use and how. For instance, if a central bank has instrument independence, a president cannot use their political influence to coerce a central bank into lowering interest rates, even if the president believes this is the best way to achieve the goals of monetary policy. If a central bank has goal independence, it has the power to choose what objectives it is attempting to achieve with its instruments. It can make judgements about what the most desirable economic outcome is, and then it can use its instrument independence to achieve these objectives. 



The Federal Reserve's institutional arrangement can help us distinguish these two forms of independence. Federal Reserve Chair Jerome \textcite{powell_panel_2023} applied Debelle and Fischer's framework to the Fed in a speech. In the 1977 amendment to the Federal Reserve Act, Congress established that the Federal Reserve has a tripartite mandate to achieve the goals of ``maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates" through its monetary policy. The Federal Reserve coloquially refers to this as the ``dual mandate" of stable prices and maximum employment, dropping the moderate long-term interest rate component because of its ``redundancy" \parencite[15]{downey_our_2025}\footnote{I will address this further in my analysis section}. Powell then argues that ``The existence of the dual mandate reflects the fact that the Federal Reserve's monetary policy independence corresponds to operational, or instrument, independence, rather than goal independence." 

%In this paper, when I refer to central bank independence (CBI), I am referring to instrument independence alone. I will explicitly distinguish goal and instrument independence when necessary as I construct my argument.


The theoretical foundation for central bank independence in the economics literature lies in a few seminal works.  \textcite{kydland_rules_1977} were the first to apply the time-inconsistency and credible commitment public policy problem to the inflation-unemployment tradeoff detailed in economics literature. In brief, in a model economy with rational agents and a government seeking to maximize some ``social objective function," governments will naturally have a temptation to implement a surprise expansionary (inflationary) policy that temporarily pushes down unemployment \parencite[473]{kydland_rules_1977}. If the economy is booming an unemployment is low, then politicians will have a higher chance of winning elections. However, rational agents in the economy predict this action, so they set their inflation expectations higher. This means that a surprise action the government would have to take would need to be even larger to create an unexpected decline in unemployment, triggering even higher inflation. This principle makes credible commitment by governments challenging regarding economic policy, so the end result is heightened inflation expectations. Consequently, inflation becomes anchored at a level that is higher than what the social objective function deems optimal.

\textcite{barro_rules_1983} apply this logic directly to central banking. First, note that they (along with much other theoretical literature on monetary policy) assume that monetary policy is neutral in the long run, meaning that changing the money supply (amount of currency in circulation) does not influence real economic output after a sufficiently long period of time. Most simply, if the amount of currency in circulation increases, the relative price between goods should, in theory, also increase, so output remains unchanged \parencite[49-50]{downey_our_2025}. Given this, \textcite{barro_rules_1983} suggests that monetary policy can only influence the economy if there is a short-run surprise, before prices have time to readjust. A surprise increase in the money supply (expansionary monetary policy) can lead to some short-run expansion of economic output, though eventually, prices will simply rise and output will return to its original level. Under the logic of the time inconsistency problem, rational agents predict attempts to stimulate the economy through monetary policy by political agents. Thus, they have high inflation expectations, and this anchors observed inflation at a heightened level without reaping any economic rewards. 

\textcite{barro_rules_1983} argue that the optimal setup is a central bank that is entirely constrained by pre-established rules strictly governing monetary policy decisions, ensuring that central bankers are not tempted to loosen monetary policy and trigger inflation. This establishes credibility in the central bank, lowering inflation expectations. However, rules may not be a perfect arrangement. In the real world, it may be important for a central bank to respond to unique, changing economic situations, and rules are intrinsically inflexible. \textcite[1177]{rogoff_optimal_1985} provide several possible solutions. The most famous solution is the appointing of a ``conservative central banker" who is ``known to place a greater weight on inflation stabilization" than the general public would, on its own, desire. In particular, Rogoff's conservative central banker ``places a larger weight on inflation relative to employment stabilization" \parencite[2]{romelli_trends_2024}. Additionally, a conservative central banker must be free to set monetary policy without political interference. Otherwise, even if their own preferences are unchanged, the public will expect political agents to coerce the central banker into inflating, undoing the positive effect of lowered inflation expectations. Thus, independence is uniquely tied into conceptions of the conservative central banker.
%Mention both forms of independence here?

Central bank independence has become the dominant form of monetary policymaking around the world \parencite{crowe_evolution_2007}. This process occurred over a period of decades following the end of World War II, but much modern analysis of CBI begins with the late 1970s/early 1980s Federal Reserve. During the stagflation episode of the late 1970s, where oil supply shocks meant high inflation occurred during a period of heightened employment, Paul Volcker assumed became Fed Chair in 1979. He chose explicitly to prioritize combatting inflation over unemployment, despite pressure from President Reagan to lower interest rates. During his tenure, the Fed raised interest rates significantly, putting the economy in a recession while simultaneously bringing down inflation \parencite{goodfriend_incredible_2005}. This episode is frequently referenced in the economics literature for two reasons. First, it serves as a simple empirical example of the time inconsistency problem, where a politician wanted interest rates lower than what was optimal, and an inflation-averse, ``conservative" central banker resolved the problem by emphasizing price stability \parencite{mishkin_central_2008, goodfriend_incredible_2005}. Second, the Federal Reserve technocrats used their special expertise to successfully combat an inflation episode. Even though there was short-term pain in the economy, the literature argues that the US reaped the long-term benefits \parencite[151]{alesina_central_1993}. This helped provide legitimacy to the technocrats of central banks holding unique, strong monetary policy powers. Under these assumptions, CBI became the institutionally accepted arrangement for central banking around the world \parencite{crowe_evolution_2007}. ``Central bank independence has achieved an almost taken-for-granted quality in contemporary political life, with little questioning of its logic or effectiveness" \parencite[47]{mcnamara_rational_2002}.

\subsection{Political Literature on Central Banking}

\label{sec:political_sci_lit}

I now turn to the political science and political literature on CBI. There is a well-established body of work questioning the assumptions underpinning central bank independence. I focus on academic pieces whose content differs from the assumptions made in the previously discussed economics literature. This is important context for my analysis sections, where I analyze the methodology of some core economics papers.


I start with a general commentary on normative vs positive assumptions. Economics modeling generally attempts to discover the set of economic conditions necessary to maximize social welfare, given a series of assumptions. Measuring human welfare itself is normative concept. There is no absolute, definitive measure of welfare; how economists determine what maximized welfare is requires normative assessments based on values and personal beliefs \parencite{cairney_understanding_2011}. Rational agents with different values can easily disagree on measurements of welfare. Who gets to make these value assessments is important, particularly given that monetary policy's implementation does not impact all citizens equally \parencite{stiglitz_price_2013}. Thus, even if a model is produced technically correctly, its prescribed economy can differ from those desired by different political agents without either party being incorrect. They simply have a difference in values.


One important source of technocratic bias in monetary policymaking may come from the literature they consume. \textcite{weiss_many_1979} describes an ``enlightenment" model where officials are influenced by the bulk, underlying narratives that show up in a body of academic work. Over time, research can become ``engrained" in public policy decisions, changing the way problems are framed. Given this, it is worth understanding the literature that influences policymakers at the Federal Reserve the most, and the implicit biases in that literature. This idea brings us back to where we started. Given that there is no such thing as an ``apolitical: arrangement, the norms and values that underpin an institutional, public policy setup that get defined as ``normal" and ``apolitical" are worth understanding. Literature contributes to this process by establishing core ideas as ``settled fact," pushing research and policy in a particular direction. In the case of central banking, institutional ``amnesia" occurred, and policymakers ``forget" that the arrangement they embody was different and, therefore, could be different in the future \parencite{mcnamara_rational_2002}. 


I now turn to the economics methodologies itself. A core assumption that underpins much of the modeling in the economics literature is that monetary policy is neutral in the long-run. This has its roots in a very simplistic model of the implementation of changes to the money supply. In practice, the Federal Reserve has no power to influence the money supply directly; it is banks who adjust their lending based on the supply and demand of currency \parencite{watson_institutional_2002}. Rather than money supply being adjusted equally for all individuals at the same time, it is adjusted through influencing the lending decisions of banks. Thus, there is a double delegation of monetary policy under CBI: from the legislature to the central bank, and from the central bank to banks \parencite{downey_our_2025}. Given this, the functioning of monetary policy is tied to the prosperity of the financial sector, implying that monetary policy decisions can have important distributional consequences \parencite{jacobs_fed_2021}. For instance, the large scale asset purchases and the establishment of positive interest on excess reserves following the 2008 recession particularly benefit the banking sector and those with access to assets. Which banks can participate in lending facilities or trade directly with the Federal Reserve have important, lasting consequences. Monetary neutrality cannot apply to this more complex world of monetary policy, where complex instruments are utilized and implemented through private banks \parencite{downey_our_2025}.


An additional assumption supporting independence regards the ability of central bankers to execute monetary policy effectively and without bias, ensuring that their policies maximize welfare across the economy. However, there is little evidence that any policymaker or technocrat, much less monetary policy makers, can serve as such unbiased bureaucrat. All human beings suffer basic cognitive limitations that bias judgement and limit the effectiveness of policy, and monetary policy technocrats are no different \parencite{simon_chapter_1997, thaler_nudge_2008}. In particular, career incentives and professional socialization routinely cloud judgement of central bankers \parencite{adolph_bankers_2013}. For instance, if a monetary policymaker built their career in the banking sector, they tend to defend policies that allow banks to take on more leverage \parencite{wirsching_revolving_2018}. If an economist has been socialized under an economic doctrine that defines optimal economic welfare in a certain way, they are likely to also see the world this way \parencite{jacobs_fed_2021}.  

%Given the double delegation of policy, it seems natural that many central bankers have their roots in finance. Central banks and financial institutions work closely together, so there is an understanding of the financial system that can be beneficial. This can also be harmful. \textcite{adolph_agents_2013} documents how institutional leaders with professional ties to finance are more supportive of tighter monetary policies that deliver lower inflation but higher unemployment.\footnote{I describe in 4.1 why finance may prefer price stability as an objective of monetary policy.} Additionally, many policymakers contain the implicit view that that a prosperous financial system is necessary for the economy. Since the Volcker-era Federal Reserve, the growth of the financial sector has rapidly outpaced that of the rest of the US economy. Buoyed on Fed policies, the financial sector was the first to recover from both the 2008 recession and the 2020 recession. This preference matches the institutional necessity for finance to be functioning for monetary policy to be function (the double delegation of monetary policy). 






%Additionally, episodes like the 2008 recession and the post-COVID spike in inflation highlight how the Fed's technocratic justification may be imperfect. Like all agencies, it makes mistakes, but in a world of independence, it politically holds itself to a higher bar. Yet, Federal Reserve officials have demonstraterd significant shortcomings. 



Much economics literature on CBI implies that politicians cannot be entrusted with monetary policy (this is a core assumption regarding inflation and the time inconsistency problem). This implies that the creation of central bank independence, and the norms that are associated with it, are outside of the political process. However, central bank independence did not materialize out of thin air; it occurred through a political process \parencite{mcnamara_rational_2002}. The powers of central banks and the goals of monetary policy definitially were allocated through to the political process. The determination of socially ``optimal" monetary policy is also a political question.

Political scientists even contend that the proliferation of CBI as the dominant institutional regime for central banking may be tied to seeking political legitimacy and disempowering future politicians who may have different political objectives \parencite{dellepiane-avellaneda_gordon_2013}. The Bank of England's establishment as an independent central bank in the mid-1990s serves as a prominant example of this. Finally, the time inconsistency problem applies to a variety of fiscal policies \parencite{berman_bank_1999}. One could imagine a situation where politicians could pass irresponsible economic policy, like large tax cuts or direct payments to citizens, immediately before elections. It is not immediately obvious why monetary policy should be distinguished from other economic policymaking.



Criticisms of central bank independence have been met with rhetoric regarding the dangers of hyperinflation. The possibility of having any political influence on monetary policy is met with strong criticisms \parencite{woodruff_democratize_2019}. One political science theory that may help explain this dynamic comes from \textcite{lindblom_market_1982}. As the role of the financial sector grew, so did the perceived costs of policies that could possibly hamper its growth. Even if that growth was suboptimal, policies protecting those not benefitting from this growth could be perceived to damage the economy. \textcite{lindblom_market_1982} likens a situation like this to a prison, where it becomes extremely difficult to justify policies that cause economic pain, even if in the long-run they create improved social welfare. This entrenches the current status of regimes and institutions and prevents reform.


There are consequences to entirely removing monetary policy from the political process, where independence results in politicians being unable to effectively act on macroeconomic issues. One such area of concern regards the lack of knowledge of politicians on monetary policy and macroeconomic conditions more broadly. Most simply, the disempowerment of the legislature means polticians no longer have to be educated about it \parencite{downey_our_2025}. By distancing itself from the legislature, Congresspeople do not have to understand core areas of the Federal Reserve's responsibilities. There is no real requirement for them to contribute to Fed policy, and CBI provides a technical justification for them not to learn about monetary policy. It is simply outside of their control, and it is outside of their control for the good of the nation. This became apparent in 2015, when in \textcite{bernanke_courage_2017}, Chair Bernanke describes how he misconstrued the mechanism for the Federal Reserve to create reserves to establish an emergency loan to AIG, during the 2008 crisis, because he did not have the time to explain to a senator the technicalities of central banking. Thus, the removal of the political process from central banking, in its entirety, has adverse consequences to the political process itself. 

Central bank independence also has the biproduct of making fiscal policy less and less important for macroeconomic conditions relative to monetary policy. Some describe this phenomenon as central bank ``loneliness" \parencite{mabbett_independent_2019}. Decisions with distributional or normative consequences are traditionally handled by politicians in a functioning democratic society. Voters theoretically can exercise their frustrations or support in the ballot box, ensuring that the policies implemented have their perspectives in mind\footnote{The question of whether the United States is a functioning democracy is beyond the scope of this paper. However, the scope of this paper handles whether it is good for voters to be unable to influence the goals of monetary policy.}. CBI challenges this by entrusting core macroeconomic performance on an institution that is beyond the scope of the political process \parencite{hopkin_global_2019}. There is no mechanism for voters to push reform, even if that institution is not prioritizing the normative goals deemed most important by voters \parencite{woodruff_democratize_2019}. If the economy is performing poorly, Congress can simply blame the Fed without taking any real action \parencite{mabbett_independent_2019}. There are no longer significant political repercussions for bad monetary policy, removing one source of potential political turmoil from politicians \parencite{woodruff_democratize_2019}. This means Congress has little incentive to act on the economy. Action creates the possibility of voter discontent and ultimately bad performance in elections; inaction prevents such from occurring. As a consequence, independence results in central banks being the primary actors of macroeconomic policy.



\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This paper provides a methodological analysis of the CBI economics literature and how it influences central bankers. I seek to understand the papers that developed the arguments regarding central bank independence and how these papers influence the way policymakers communicate about CBI. First, I consider wheter the economics literature conflates instrument and goal independence by presupposing which goals central banks attempt to achieve. In particular, I hypothesize that price stability is regularly considered the dominant monetary policy goal, and other goals are not considered in the literature. This is a falsifiable hypothesis; if economics literature considers other possible goals of monetary policy beyond price stability, then the hypothesis fails. Additionally, a rival hypothesis could also exist. Independence may have been developed as a biproduct of central bank's attempts to fight inflation. In particular, if monetary policy cannot influence real economic variables in the long-run outside of inflation, then inflation is the only meaningful political goal for monetary policy. It is not necessary to consider alternative options, because they would make no sense in this policy space.

%I trace the epistemological assumptions that underpin the theoretical models of central banks into the empirical literature. This literature is not US-specific; theoretical modeling is not country-specific, and the empirical literature tends to do cross-country analysis of CBI. However, I focus on the links between research and policymakers using speeches by Federal Reserve governors. Given that the United States is one of the largest economies in the world, and central bank independence around the world was adopted and developed in large part following the lead of the United States in the late 1970s-1980s, using them as a case study makes sense. It is worth also noting that the Federal Reserve is a research institution as well as the conductor of monetary policy. Each year, Fed economists publish many leading papers, and they lead dialogue on a variety of macroeconomic research subjects. To understand the intersection between economics research and monetary policy, the natural starting point is the Fed.


My analysis is built into two subcomponents. The first section, \ref{sec:analysis_papers_all}, studies the way core arguments in the economics literature were developed. To do this, in \ref{sec:analysis_theoretical_models}, I qualitatively analyze the methodologies of some of the most well-cited academic economics papers, many of which build on each other, to discover the root assumptions that underpin these models. I consider the origins for price stability as a goal of monetary policy, the conservative central banker model, and why this model was defended in the literature. I then move to the empirical analysis in section \ref{sec:analysis_empiricial_cbi_indices}, where I study how the findings of the theoretical papers are incorporated in definitions of central bank independence. In order to study how the conflation of instrument and goal independence bias indices that measure de jure, or legal, central bank independence, I compare the US and T\"urkiye as an illuminative example. In both subsections, I study whether the economics literature on CBI allows for the legislature to define the goals of monetary policy, or if the goals of monetary policy are presupposed by the literature. If the literature dynamically considers different possibilities for the goals of monetary policy, then the literature successfully distinguishes between goal and instrument independence. If it only considers one set of goals, or penalizes central banks for having different goals, then goal and instrument independence are conflated.

In \ref{sec:analysis_empiricial_cbi_indices}, I trace the epistemological prior in the economics literature into the monetary policymaking arena through Federal Reserve governor speeches. The economics literature is not US-specific; theoretical modeling is not country-specific, and the empirical literature tends to do cross-country analysis of CBI. However, given that the United States is one of the largest economies in the world, and central bank independence around the world was adopted and developed in large part following the lead of the United States in the late 1970s-1980s, using it as a case study makes sense.  Federal Reserve governors make speeches to communicate important ideas about monetary policy and the institution. Thus, looking at the way they communicate about economic research, and what they choose to emphasize, is informative in understanding how the epistemological prior in the academic literature influences policymakers. To accomplish this, I imported a total of 2,803 speeches and public statements from 1996 to 2025 by Federal Reserve officials. I originally the metadata for the speeches from repositories stored on the platforms, Kaggle and Huggingface. These two platforms allow users to build publicly available, open-source repositories using artificial intelligence. Kaggle's data exists between 1996-2020, while Huggingface's data is from 1996-2025. I merged the two datasets together to verify accuracy, and I then filtered these speeches to those that reference some of the core economics literature. On speeches that handled subject matter I was interested in, I pulled quotes and references directly from their online location, published by the Federal Reserve. Thus, I did not rely on Kaggle or Huggingface for individual speech transcripts, but rather, as a starting point for my analysis.





\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis_all}

\subsection{Epistemological Assumptions in the Economics Literature}
\label{sec:analysis_papers_all}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Economics Modeling}
\label{sec:analysis_theoretical_models}



Economics modeling on the time inconsistency problem assumes that society prefers a certain overall inflation rate, but it is not always obvious why. For instance, \textcite[475]{kydland_rules_1977} begins by defining an ``agreed-upon social objective function" that is ``assumed to exist," in which society determines the inflation rate that is most desirable. The paper then proceeds to solve for maximum welfare, given this function. \textcite[52]{downey_our_2025} notes how previous Fed Chair Ben Bernanke states, ``In the long run, the central bank can affect only inflation, and not real variables such as output." If the price for of goods increases, but buying power also increases (wages increase), consumers can purchase just as many goods as before. It is not always apparent why inflation should be included in some social objective function in the first place, and most models of CBI do not specifically challenge this. \textcite[58-61]{downey_our_2025} critiques the idea that there are general costs to society of inflation. In most cases, the costs of readjusting prices for businesses are small, particularly given most prices can easily be readjusted on the web. While there can be costs associated with surprise inflation, where businesses make improper projections about their operating costs, these costs generally are small relative to other economic problems. \footnote{In the interest of time, I generally ignore hyperinflation in this essay, but it is worth noting that there are plenty of historical examples of non-dependent central banks not experiencing hyperinflation, including the United States in the three decades following WWII.} Thus, if inflation were uniform and effected all agents in the economy equally, little would change. If prices increased, wages would also rise, and overall consumption would remain unchanged. 

However, inflation is not some uniform economic phenomenon; it affects various interest groups differently. First, its worth noting that the Federal Reserve meets the price stability component of its dual mandate by targeting 2\% inflation. This is measured primarily through the ``core" (excluding food and gas) Personal Consumption Expenditures index produced by the Bureau of Economic Analysis. It estimates inflation by building a basket of goods reflecting general consumption behavior, and measuring how prices have changed for the goods within that basket. Measuring inflation through a single index that covers the entire economy misses the heterogeneity of consumption habits, however. Different socioeconomic groups consume differently, and thus price changes for certain goods and services matter differentially. Federal Reserve officials emphasize the role that inflation can play for the poor, where families barely have enough consumption power to make ends meet, and they use this to justify their monetary policy decisionmaking. If a family is living paycheck-to-paycheck, and prices increase more than their paycheck, they may be unable to pay for essential goods and services. Meanwhile, wealthier families can use their savings or investment income the minimize the damage of inflation. Thus, it would be useful for the Federal Reserve to measure the erosion of consumption power for the poor and the influence of policy on this erosion. This, currently, is not part of the Federal Reserve's objectives.

An important additional normative question surrounds the costs of unemployment. Economic theory posits that, in the long-run, tightening monetary policy does not change real variables; it only lowers inflation. However, in the short-run, unemployment rises temporarily. This expansion of unemployment does not randomly occur; those at the bottom of the income and wealth latter (who are most vulnerable to an adverse financial shock) are also those who are most likely to experience unemployment triggered by the business cycle. A cyle of unemployment can lead to financial ruin for these families, impacting them long after (as the theory of monetary neutrality suggests) the economy has returned to its original output in long-run. Thus, there are concentrated costs to even short-run unemployment that have lasting consequences for predominantly lower-income families. 


Setting aside this heterogeneity, there is a real, measurable cost to inflation for creditors (particularly banks) relative to debtors. A core feature of bank intermediation is maturity transformation, where banks take cash (deposits) and other short-term investments and make long-term loans (mortgages, corporate loans, etc...). These loans generally have a fixed interest rate. If inflation spikes, the real value of these loans falls; each dollar a debt repays the creditor becomes worth less than when it was borrowed. Here, an aggregate measure of inflation becomes more appropriate to use. Banks distribute money into the entire economy, so the average value of a given dollar influences the ability for banks to continue lending, based on the recouped gains from previous loans. Given that there are varying costs to inflation for different interest and demographic groups, ``inflation has significant redistributive effects" \textcite[256]{posen_declarations_1995}.


Given this, it is clear that how we measure inflation, and why we care about inflation, varies significantly. Different socioeconomic groups consume differing baskets of goods and differing costs to inflation and unemployment. Yet the theoretical literature on monetary policy begins its modeling under the core assumption that there is some social objective function that reflects the aggregate preferences of society. In particular, \textcite[480]{kydland_rules_1977} describe a ``socially preferred inflation rate is zero, which seems consistent with the public's preferences," and ``a more informed public might prefer some  positive or negative inflation." \textcite[103]{barro_rules_1983} acknowledge ``distortions from income taxation, unemployment compensation, and the like," yet ignores these distortions in the interior model. While, they think ``of the monetary authority’s objective as reflecting the preferences of the “representative’ private agent," they ``express this objective as a function of actual and expected rates of inflation" \parencite[102]{barro_rules_1983}. \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985} extends the modeling implications that are predicated off having one uniform, socially optimal inflation rate. In all cases, the differential impacts of inflation on different interest groups, or even how we measure inflation, is left out of the model. 

The premise of the conservative central banker is a central banker who is more inflation-averse than the public. Yet, Rogoff leaves an underlying question unanswered: How might a central banker obtain this anti-inflation bias? What influences might lead to such an outcome? One possible answer is that central bankers, as technocrats, are influenced by the economics literature and the ``facts" of monetary policy. They understand technically what monetary policy is and why it matters, far more than the public or political figures. The economics literature itself thus becomes self-reinforcing: central bankers believe the models of economics literature and believe they are optimal for society, so they follow them. However, the literature described in \ref{sec:political_sci_lit} addresses both the biases of central bankers intrinsic in policymaking and the limits of technocratic justification for the democratic delegation of monetary policymaking. 

A second possible answer is that special interest groups who uniquely benefit more from lower inflation relative to the rest of the public can help enforce central bank independence. In particular, \textcite[256]{posen_declarations_1995} argues that increasing CBI "is taken to be equivalent to increasing the relative weight of price stability over employment goals in the monetary authority's utility function," and that this "conforms with the conservative central banker of Rogoff (1985)." Posen identifies this interest group as the financial sector: ``the financial sector is uniquely positioned to provide that support, and central banks have been most independent where that support has been strongest" \parencite[254]{posen_declarations_1995}. 



%%%We can mention IOER and negative rates if we want to
It is worth noting that this story, in the ``real" world, is complicated. We have already established how monetary policy may not be neutral in the long-run, and it also has obtained increased complexity over the last four decades. \textcite{wansleben_rise_2023} establishes how part of the Federal Reserve's growth over the last 40 years has revolved around policy innovations that make expansionary monetary policy non-harmful (or even beneficial to banks). I do not fully address this complexity in this paper. However, the simplistic models that underpins the conservative central banker also do not address these complexities. In a world of simple monetary policy, with a simple toolkit and a simple lever, there is strong evidence that financial institutions particularly benefit from tighter monetary policy that combats unexpected inflation. It is this simple model that is used in the core theoretical models on the time inconsistency problem and, thus, central bank independence.

The expansion of monetary policy's power, combined with the low interest rate environment since 2008 complicates the picture that finance exclusively benefits from the weighting of price stability over the employment in monetary policy goals. Large scale asset purchases are the immediate example of this, because ``by recapitalizing the financial sector and not the broader economy, QE had a political multiplier effect that enhanced the political influence of banks at the expense of ordinary citizens" \textcite[738]{adolph_missing_2018}. The core takeaway from this subsection is not that finance uniformly benefits from tight monetary policy. Instead, the development of the theoretical literature that justifies central bank independence, and thus, modern western monetary governance, is predicated on a model that explicitly overvalued a normative assumption that benefited financial institutions. Their interests were modeled as the interests of finance through the development of the ``conservative central banker," while the complexities of the distributional consequences of inflation were left out of monetary policy. In the next section, I demonstrate how the assumptions, which encoded the interests of finance, heavily influenced the empirical literature measuring the efficacy of CBI.



\subsubsection{Empirical Evidence and CBI Indices}
\label{sec:analysis_empiricial_cbi_indices}



Economics literature has attempted to measure the influence of central bank independence through empirical analysis. Regarding both goal and instrument independence, it can be difficult to measure the level of influence of political figures on central bank decisions. Thus, many empirical papers distinguish between ``de jure" and ``de facto" indepndence \parencite{romelli_political_2022}. While de facto analysis attempts to analyze the personal influence of political figures on central banking, de jure measures ground themselves in the law surrounding monetary governance, from appointment of central bank officials to the laws establishing the goals of monetary policy. I emphasize de jure independence in this paper because the assumptions in determining what an ``independent" central bank truly means are informative. These assumptions are explicitly described in the methodologies of de jure papers, but because de facto papers measure things like political influence, they have less commentary on the central bank institutionally.

Empirical measurement of de jure CBI generally involves simplifying the laws governing a central bank into an index. Authors study different components of central bank governance, and determine the degree to which the law describes an independent central bank. Indices define perfect de jure CBI as ``1" and complete de jure central bank dependence as ``0." Arguing for the merits of a given index or strategy is beyond the scope of this paper, but they all share a basic theoretical basis in the arguments initially presented by \textcite{kydland_rules_1977}, \textcite{barro_rules_1983}, and \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985}. These empirical papers generally find one result, namely that the politicization of monetary policy raises inflation without boosting economic growth \parencite[4]{posen_introduction_2025}. 

%I feel like this is an important paragraph, so we want to return to it
In the coming paragraphs, I will produce analysis of various CBI papers to argue that a core feature of this literature is the conflation of goal and operational independence. Under the \textcite{fischer_central-bank_1995} model, even though instrument independence is optimal, the goals of monetary policy should be entirely set through political processes. The type of goals a central bank is set to achieve should thus not be relevant to a CBI index if that index is principally measuring instrument independence. In particular, a codified ``price stability" target is often incorporated as part of the optimal arrangement of an independent central bank. This may be optimal for achieving the conservative central banker vision of monetary policy, but the definition itself has little to do with how independent a central bank is.

Some of the most influential works on de jure CBI are \textcite{bade_central_1988}, \textcite{alesina_credibility_1988},  \textcite{grilli_political_1991}, and \textcite{alesina_central_1993}. \textcite{alesina_credibility_1988} builds on a methodology developed by \textcite{bade_central_1988} to create one of the earliest measures of de jure independence. They decompose forms of independence into ``political" and ``financial" independence. Political independence generally deals with insulation of central bank officials from political processes (like appointment and firing protections and the government approval of monetary policy decisions) to achieve monetary policy objectives, while financial independence emphasizes how the central bank is legally obligated to finance the government. While \textcite{bade_central_1988} consider both forms of independence, \textcite{alesina_credibility_1988} focuses almost exclusively on political independence. The codified goals of monetary policy are not explicitly included in these definitions.


This changes with \textcite[366]{grilli_political_1991}, where ``political independence" is defined as ``the capacity to choose the final goal of monetary policy." This is distinct from economic independence, or the ``capacity to choose the instruments with which to pursue these goals." Definitially, political independence can be mapped as a precursor to \textcite{fischer_central-bank_1995}'s goal independence, while economic independence is similar to instrument independence. However, \textcite[366-367]{grilli_political_1991} explicitly penalizes central banks that do not have a strict price stability target in the creation of their index. They note that ``in principle, independence to choose the final goals can be defined without reference to the contents of such goals," yet ``the main virtue of having an independent central bank is that it can provide credibility. This is why we identify independence with autonomy to pursue the goal of low inflation. Any institutional feature that enhances the central bank capacity to pursue this goal will, on our definition, increase central bank independence." In essence, they acknowledge that they are conflating goal and instrument independence, but because of the basic findings of the time inconsistency problem, there is no alternative, viable goal for monetary policy.


\textcite{alesina_central_1993} produces a statistical model measuring how different levels of CBI influence economic variables like output and inflation, based on an average measurement of the degree of independence described by \textcite{alesina_credibility_1988} and \textcite{grilli_political_1991}. They find that ``while central bank independence promotes price stability, it has no measurable impact on real economic performance" \textcite[151]{alesina_central_1993}. Additionally, there is ``a near perfect negative correlation between inflation and central bank independence" \textcite[153]{alesina_central_1993}. This finding has been cited thousands of times throughout the economics literature. Most importantly, \textcite{alesina_central_1993} accept how, for an independent central bank,``the 'price stability' objective is explicitly and prominently part of central bank statute" as an essential part of the definition of political independence. The findings the \textcite{alesina_central_1993} have been cited thousands of times in the economics literature.


Tangentially, \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} produced an expanded index on de jure CBI. They included more countries and more components than previous works, and as a consequence, this work has been repeatedly extended. More modern economics literature has built upon and modfied the indices produced by \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992}, including, but not limited to, \textcite{romelli_political_2022}, \textcite{romelli_trends_2024}, \textcite{dincer_central_2024}, and \textcite{garriga_revisiting_2025}. Alex Cukierman also extended this work in a variety of peer-reviewed work (I expand on some of these papers further in section \ref{sec:gov_speeches_analysis}. While these papers produce their own indices, they all follow the same basic framework. In other words, \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992}'s methodology is the root of most modern measures of de jure CBI. 

Crucially, \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} did not distinguish between the political and economic independence components when building its measures of de jure independence. However, like \textcite{grilli_political_1991}, they include the objectives component of independence as a component of the index, explicitly penalizing central banks who's objectives do not solely prioritize price stability.  The inclusion of a goal component here is also linked explicitly back to the previously described theoretical literature: ``In \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985}'s terminology, the objectives variable measures the strength of the 'conservative bias' of the bank's charter" \parencite[357]{cukierman_measuring_1992}. The conservative central banker was officially synonymous with CBI, methodologically speaking. This decision is paramount; empirical analysis of de jure CBI essentially stopped distinguishing between goal and instrument independence in their methodologies therafter.

The Federal Reserve's CBI scores are universally penalized, under this methodology, because its goals include more than simply price stabilty. Recall from section \ref{sec:background_economics} that the United States legally has a ``tripartite" monetary policy mandate which it coloqially refers to as its ``dual mandate." The aforementioned de jure measures in \textcite{grilli_political_1991} and \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} of CBI code the Federal Reserve's monetary policies as the ``dual mandate," resulting in a significant hit to the Federal Reserve's overall index score. Including an employment component does not match the conservative central banker model that is codified in the objectives component of these indices.


The conflation of goal and instrument independence has implications in the de jure measurement of CBI today. Using a comparison of the United States and T\"urkiye as a case study can be illuminative. ``The political system in T\"urkiye has been rapidly centralizing around the executive branch of the state, while the power of independent state institutions eroded" since 2011 \parencite[62]{demiralp_erosion_2019}. This antidemocratic trend has coincided with President Erdo\u{g}an increasing pressure on the CBRT to maintain low interest rates \parencite{gurkaynak_consequences_2023}. Inflation spiked in 2018 in T\"urkiye, particularly following a constitutional reform that dramatically increased President Erdo\u{g}an's political power. Following the reform, the CBRT fully implemented President Erdo\u{g}an's unorthodox view that lower interest rates reduces (rather than increases) inflation, on average. Figure \ref{fig:turkishinflation} displays Turkish inflation from 1990-2024. Between 2003 and 2016, Turkish inflation remained below ten percent. From 2017 on, the rate rose to above 10 percent, and between 2020 and 2022, the rate spikes from 12 to 72 percent.

With some caveats, the Federal Reserve's ``institutional strength is well established," meaning that the Turkish erosion of CBI did not occur in the United States, despite President Trump's pressure \textcite[542]{cakmakli_financial_2024}.

First, it is worth noting that modern de jure indices, despite being largely based on the core framework from \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} and/or \textcite{grilli_political_1991}, do make a series of subjective coding and methodology decisions. All of the aforementioned indices measure the CBRT as being more independent than the Federal Reserve. Tables \ref{tab:romelli_cbi}, \ref{tab:garriga_us_cbi}, and \ref{tab:dincer_cbi} show a comparison of how the United States Federal Reserve scores on de jure independence measures relative to CBI. At face value, this makes sense, given what these indices are measuring. The CBRT's mandate was revised following the Turkish currency crisis in 2001, and it was written to match the norms of central bank independence that is now well established in countries around the world. Thus, if these indices were measuring how well a law chartering a central bank encapsulates the international norms associated with CBI, the Turkish laws fit perfectly.

I argue that de jure CBI indices are biased measures of independence, as defined as instrument and goal independence, for two reasons. First, these measures leave out the strength of the institutions enforcing the laws of the central bank. On paper, the de jure independence of the CBRT has not changed in recent years, yet the expansion of presidential power (and subsequent decline in democracy in Turkey) is left out. Indeed, while XXX mentions that there is a direct correlation between democracy and CBI, virtually all papers ignore this for practical puproses. It is worth noting that the expansion of the trend of CBI, as a political maneuver, was largely based on the understanding that CBI enhances the credibility of the party that establishes it. The US was at the core of the rapid growth of CBI, as it used its international influence to entice other nations to follow its lead. However, the laws in the US themselves do not reflect the broader independence that the Federal Reserve has, because despite these indices' findings, the US is simply seen as more independent than most other central banks.

Secondly, the inclusion of an objectives component significantly biases de jure CBI indices based on the content of the goals of monetary policy. To reiterate, the content of the legal goals of monetary policy are not relevant when measuring independence; the ability for a central bank to choose its goals and pursue its goals without political pressure are. Despite this, the `objectives" component gives the biggest gap between the two nations for \textcite{romelli_trends_2024} and the second most for \textcite{garriga_revisiting_2025}. While weighting schemes for developing the indices vary by author, there is consensus that having a price stablity mandate alone should weighted as important when measuring de jure CBI. For instance, \textcite[4]{eijffinger_central_2026} describes how the \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} index ``considers the conservativeness of the central bank as defined by law" through determining the degree emphasis to which ``central bank law gives to price stability," and also that ``this aspect of the index should have been given much more weight" then \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} originally provided. 


This is at odds with the theoretical distinction, which has been echoed by Federal Reserve institutional leaders, that the Federal Reserve serves at the leisure of the mandate Congress sets out for it. Consider what would happen to the Federal Reserve's de jure index scores if Congress decided to pass a law that the Federal Reserve should include reducing inequality in access to credit within its monetary policy objectives. Institutionally, nothing would have changed; the Fed would still have autonomy in deciding which tools at its disposal to use to accomplish this objective\footnote{Perhaps it could make a new lending facility providing credit for the opening of bank branches in underserved areas, as an example.} More directly, say that Congress argues that monetary policy should be constructed with the goal of serving the poor or middle class first, explicitly\footnote{They could emphasize using a basket of goods to measure inflation utilized by the poor, or to measure full employment, they could use slices of the economy that leave out the wealthy.} This action would not influence goal \textit{or} instrument independence, because Congress would still be responsible for setting monetary policy objectives (goal \textit{dependence}) and would not have changed its ability to influence individual policy decisions (instrument independence). However, the measurement of CBI in every de jure index would change.


The implication is clear. De jure central bank independence is not about whether a central bank can utilize its goals freely to meet the political goals established by law. Instead, central bank independence is measured as the closeness to the conservative central banker ideal, defined in the theoretical literature. Deviations from this ideal are explicitly penalized, by construction.

\subsection{Federal Reserve Governors' Speeches}
\label{sec:gov_speeches_analysis}
So far, we have established that the ``conservative" central banker a version of central banking that is defended in the theoretical literature because of oversimplified models on politics and the general preferences of the public. Additionally, the definition of central bank independence in empirical literature reflects the conservative central banker, not the nuanced view of distinguishing between goal and instrument independence. I know argue that the economics literature, and its epistemological priors, has influenced the way Fed policymakers communicate about independence. I make two core arguments. First, while Fed officials routinely reference the Congressional mandate that they uphold in their monetary policy, their speeches suggest that they interpret their own mandate more closely to the presentation of independence provided by the literature, where goals are not set by politicians but rather by a single understanding of the ``conservative central banker." The implication of this is that no interest group can attempt to influence the goals of monetary policy through the political process because their is no legitimate institutional alternative to the current arrangement. Second, the financial sector is no longer considered a political interest group, but rather a vessel to transmit monetary policy. It is an essential component, so their interests are baked into the institutional arrangement. 

Papers only matter if people in positions of power implement their ideas. Thus, even though I have established that the academic economics literature conflates goal and instrument independence, this only matters if that literature influences policymakers at the Federal Reserve. Four core papers that we previously discussed (\textcite{kydland_rules_1977}, \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985}, \textcite{alesina_central_1993}, and \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} are particularly well-cited, and they were all released before the start of my sample of speeches. Thus, I focus on these speeches in my qualitative analysis. To be more hollistic in my approach, I lump any references to ``Cukierman" together because many papers published by Alex Cukierman, like \textcite{cukierman_central_2002} and \textcite{cukierman_central_2008}, build upon or contain strong references to the 1992 methodology. Table \ref{tab:fed_cbi_citations} documents how many speeches a given source was referenced by a Fed official.\footnote{Additionally, my sample contains 435 independent references to the `dual mandate."} I manually read through the speeches that referenced the core literature to produce my analysis. In the rest of the section, I detail the ways these ideas were utilized by selecting a subsample of speeches to highlight, pulling from a range of Fed officials. I aim to demonstrate how the ideas in these papers has persisted in Fed discourse over time.

First, Federal Reserve officials acknowledge and support the distinction between instrument and goal independence. For instance, \textcite{powell_panel_2023} directly cites \textcite{debelle_how_1994} to explicitly to invoke that "the Federal Reserve's monetary policy independence corresponds to operational, or instrument, independence, rather than goal independence." \textcite{kugler_central_2024} reiterates this, suggesting that ``the FOMC has been handed two coequal goals" by Congress, although `` the law provides the Federal Reserve with some degree of independence in defining them in detail."

Despite this ``coequal" nature of the dual mandate, Federal Reserve officials have a strong history of referencing the conservative central banker model in their monetary policy communication. Among all the speeches that reference \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985} in table \ref{tab:fed_cbi_citations}, none strongly criticize the conservative central banker model. There are general acknowledgements of the limitations of the model, but the speeches generally endorse the core findings. Consider \textcite{bernanke_what_2004}. First, it is worth noting that the premise of this speech is to endorse the decisions made by Paul Volcker in the late 1970s. Bernanke invokes \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985} directly, stating that ``giving the central banker an objective function that differs from the true objectives of society may be useful" with regards to fighting inflation. He then suggests that ``these ideas, supported by a great deal of empirical work, have proven highly influential." In the same tradition, \textcite{powell_panel_2023} does the same, highlighting the importance of the conservative central banker model presented by \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985} as well as the empirical evidence developed by \textcite{alesina_central_1993}. \textcite{evans_feds_2011} describes how after the 2008 recession, when fears of deflation and stagnation were dominant, many policymakers who ``are conditioned by the work of Ken Rogoff" to fight inflationary tendencies in normal times needed to shift their mindset. In other words, barring a financial crisis, the default assumption is for the officials to overvalue fears about inflation first. Perhaps this is why a ``“dual mandate rarely leads to fundamentally different policy prescriptions" than a mandate emphasizing only price stability \parencite{schnabel_navigating_2026}.

The empirical work of de jure indices has also heavily influenced policymaker speeches, particularly when they justify the current institutional arrangement of the Federal Reserve. That is not to say, though, that policymakers have not pointed out the inconsistency of including goal independence in the de jure CBI measurement schemes, as we have established in section \ref{sec:empirical_ev_cbi_indices}. Governor Laurence Meyer is one of the first policymakers to explicitly reference de jure independence index. First, in \textcite{meyer_politics_2000}, he describes how, under the methodology of \textcite{grilli_political_1991} and \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992}, when ``price stability is the exclusive objective of monetary policy," a central bank scores better, so the Fed is penalized for having a dual mandate. He explicitly prefers \textcite{bade_central_1988}'s measure because of this flaw. However, it is worth noting that \textcite{meyer_monetary_1996} interprets the Fed's dual mandate as primarily a price stability mandate, at least in the long-run under the doctrine of monetary neutrality.\footnote{As I describe in section \ref{sec:political_sci_lit}, this doctrine may not reflect modern monetary policymaking's complexities}. Thus, there is a tension between the two speeches. Economic theory would defend the construction of the indices because monetary policy only matters for inflation in the long-run. 

Additional speeches acknowledge the tension regarding the dual mandate and de jure CBI indices. This conversation centers around \textcite{cukierman_central_2008}, where Cukierman extended his 1992 work. Note that \textcite{cukierman_central_2008} uses a variety of caveats to the broader findings about the relationship between inflation and CBI, particularly regarding developing vs developed countries, and points to literature that questions the relationship between inflation and employment. Also, note that \textcite[723]{cukierman_central_2008} uses ``conservativeness and independence interchangeably" since they are extremely related and considers price stability is ``the primary responsibility" of a central bank. \textcite{fischer_central_2015} and \textcite{kugler_central_2024} both point out this tension, and criticize the literature for blurring the lines between instrument and goal independence. \textcite{kugler_central_2024} reference \textcite{romelli_trends_2024} to highlight this problem. Still, other Federal Reserve officials appeared to use the independence measures include the price stability objective. \textcite{mishkin_central_2008} uses this work to state, simply, that ``countries with the highest degree of central bank independence are found to have the best inflation performance." Similarly, \textcite{kohn_federal_2009} argues, based on \textcite{cukierman_central_2008}, that ``countries with more independent central banks experience lower and more stable rates of inflation with no sacrifice of jobs or income." 

Most starkly, \textcite{alesina_central_1993}, where much of the original conflation of goal and instrument independence became ingrained in the literature, is still prominantly referenced as a core piece of literature on CBI. My sample found 13 speeches that reference \textcite{alesina_central_1993}, 10 of which provide it as a simple, baseline citation about the merits of CBI with little additional insight. Citing it effectively checks an academic box; it is a baseline, core paper that is widely accepted. Only three speeches provide any substantive conversation on this beyond a simple citation. Then governor \textcite{powell_audit_2015} referred to the paper as ``seminal," although he describes how ``more recent research finds the relationship to be less solid... in part because independent central banks have in several cases been instituted precisely because economic performance has been subpar." \textcite{kugler_central_2024} acknowledges the finding in \textcite{alesina_central_1993} before arguing about the merits of maintaining an employment objective. Finally, Chair \textcite{powell_panel_2023} provides a reference to \textcite{alesina_central_1993} as ``empirical evidence" of the inflation merits of CBI (he later addresses the importance of maintaining instrument, not goal, independence). In all cases, the theoretical assumptions that underping \textcite{alesina_central_1993} are either ignored or not criticized. 


Thus, the economics work addressed in section \ref{sec:analysis_papers_all} are used regularly by policymakers as starting points on discussions of CBI. The papers' core findings are rarely contested, along with their normative assumptions, even if the weaknesses regarding methodologies (particularly regarding de jure CBI indices) are openly acknowledged. Instead, they are referred to as the baseline for further conversation. This theme is summarized nicely by \textcite{mishkin_central_2008}, who describes how ``special interests may try to apply pressure on the central bank to boost employment in the short term through an overly expansionary monetary policy." Mishkin validates these points through references to \textcite{alesina_central_1993}, \textcite{kydland_rules_1977}, and various works by Cukierman. Which special interests are encoded to the models in the first place is never touched. 



\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper analyzed how economics literature justifies the current institutional arrangement of central bank independence. It tracks how the conflation between goal and instrument independence in both the theoretical and empirical literature. I established how normative assumptions about social welfare functions, along with the assumption of monetary neutrality, were essential to the creation of the conservative central banker model. This model establishes price stability as the optimal social goal of central banking. Then, I argued that the emphasis on price stability is incorporated in empirical measures of de jure independence. Finally, I note how Federal Reserve policymaker speeches often acknowledge the conflation of goal and instrument independence in the literature, yet they rarely critique the findings of the papers that are predicated on this bias.

The findings of this paper should not be construed as criticizing instrument independence or defending the expansion of executive power regarding the Federal Reserve. The economics and political science literature broadly defends the implementation of a variety of policies, including monetary policy, from political influence. However, in my view, independence should not be considered absolute. The goals of monetary policy are the product of political, not economic, decisions. Ensuring that these goals reflect the changing social needs of constituents is imperative. Thus, in my view, literature can develop a better understanding of the merits of different goals of monetary policy, ensuring that the democratic process has a role to play in central banking. This can occur independently of the legal, economic and political defense of instrument central bank independence.

\textcite{powell_panel_2023} argues that the Fed ``should "stick to our knitting" and not wander off to pursue perceived social benefits that are not tightly linked to our statutory goals and authorities." Under the historical distinction of instrument and goal independence, that makes institutional sense. Sticking to the explicit goals of monetary policy, as laid out by law, meets the standard of instrument independence. However, if the literature that underpins many of the academic arguments justifying this institutional arrangement conflate goal and instrument independence, then it becomes impossible to politically challenge and improve the aforementioned statutory arrangement. Defining the goals of monetary policy is a political job; the economics literature today does not create space for analysis that can inform this political conversation.

%%%Reference lindblom? We should not consider the objective of monetary policy as a prison.
As a final thought, I argue that stifling the debate on the goals of monetary policy under the guise of ``preserving central bank independence" is, in my view, dangerous. Normative loading and the neglect of political subjects in economics literature means that the interests who are most strongly encoded in the baseline literature have an academic justification for continuing to benefit from the current institutional arrangement of monetary policy. In the era of central bank independence, inequality has rapidly expanded in the United States, driven by the unprecedented growth of the financial sector. As previously established, banking in particular has a unique relationship to the Federal Reserve. Academics who criticize this arrangement should not be considered apologists for the expansion of executive authority regarding monetary policy, nor should these arguments be shut down under the guise of leading to hyperinflation. The goals of monetary policy are intrinsically political and subject to debate. Academics should spend time analyzing a possible financial system that can address social issues like inequality without sacrificing instrument independence. I envision a world where monetary policy is free to evolve, informed by a new literature on the goals of monetary policy and driven by the democratic process. The economics and political science literature, together, can help us achieve this vision.
