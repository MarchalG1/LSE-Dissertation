\section{Introduction} 

On June 29, 2026, the Supreme Court of the United States released two different rulings regarding executive power. In \textcite[slip op. at 2, 5]{trump_slaughter_2026}, the Supreme Court dramatically argued that the US President has the power to fire heads of Congressionally-established independent agencies ``at will" and without "cause." On the same day, the Supreme Court release a ruling in Trump v. Cook. Since the start of President Trump's second term, the President has repeatedly attempted to influence the Fed to lower interest rates, using a variety of threats and political pressure. These efforts culminated in President Trump attempting to fire Governor Lisa Cook based on claims of mortgage fraud. At the time of writing this paper, these mortgage fraud claims had not been proven in court, and Governor Cook had not been given the opportunity to respond to these claims in a legal setting.

In Trump v. Cook, the Supreme Court ruled that the President could not remove Governor Cook without proving that she had committed mortgage fraud in a legal setting. While the President can remove policymakers at the Fed with sufficient ``cause," he had not demonstrated such cause regarding Governor Cook. This notably differs from the sweeping executive powers established in Trump v. Slaughter to fire heads of other agencies without cause. Thus, the Supreme Court appeared to carve out a constitutional protection for the Federal Reserve, legally distinguishing it from other agencies. To justify this, the court made references to the history of central banking in the United States. However, it also referenced arguments that are echoed in some economics literature. For instance, the court argues that ``it is the Federal Reserve’s independence that allows it to pursue its mandate of 'maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates'" \parencite[slip op. at 14]{trump_cook_2026}. This leads to an interesting question: what are the underlying academic arguments that justify this position?

Central bank independence has been extensively written about in academic settings for decades. Some of the most well-cited academic papers in the world, written by economists, describe the merits of the current institutional arrangement of America's central bank. They cite the time-inconsistency problem and core macroeconomic theory to argue that monetary policy can only be effective if it is removed from the political process. Others, predominantly political scientists and public policy academics, question this rhetoric. Some criticize central bank independence as antidemocratic, while others suggest independent central banks create policies that benefit financial institutions and enhance inequality. Through this debate, the two sets of literature rarely directly engage with each other. The economics papers generally ignore criticisms from the political science world, and vice versa. In the economics arena, independence is settled fact; in the political science arena, it is dangerous and problematic.


This paper interrogates the literature on central bank independence. I study the academic development of the core arguments of independence, from both theoretical and empirical lenses. Given that the Supreme Court ruling echoed the logic of the academic economics literature, I seek to discover the normative assumptions that underpin the economic analysis of central bank independence. In particular, I investigate the underlying modeling assumptions that are used in both theoretical and empirical analysis on central bank independence. I do not seek to justify the Supreme Court's rulings on executive power. Instead, I seek to understand the logic of what Federal Reserve independence is and why it is important, as this logic may help justify why the Federal Reserve as ``different" relative to other executive agencies. To do this, I conduct a methodological analysis of some of the most famous economics papers on independence to discover where these papers made political assumptions that influenced their ultimate findings.

Through my analysis, I discover a circular, epistemic loop that is prevelant in the economics literature. The economics literature has established the importance of distinguishing between goal and instrument independence. While instrument independence is formally endorsed by economics literature and Federal Reserve policymakers, the underlying models and empirical analysis that justify such positions often assume that the goals of monetary policy match those of the ``conservative central bank" who is more inflation-averse than the general public. The theoretical literature builds this model based on political assumptions about the harms of inflation, while the empirical literature encodes price stability mandates into the definition of independence.

Section 5 provides conclusions and discussions.

The interests of the financial sector are baked into institutional and legal definitions of central bank independence. These interests originate in the epistemological assumptions of the economics literature through the social objective functions utilized in the theoretical models (the ``conservative central banker") and empirical measurement of de jure central bank independence. The assumptions are transmitted, through personnel and discourse, into consistent blockage of legislative revision of the Fed's dual mandate. The end result is a system that reflects regulatory capture without requiring explicit coordination or lobbying among firms; private interests instead only have to promote inaction, and they can do so solely through their influence in the broader market.

The broader point: We build an argument that the economics literature that juswtifies central bank independence bakes in the preferences of the financial sector, and these papers have had an important epistemological influence on policymaking.

\section{Background and Theory}


\subsection{Economics Central Bank Independence Literature}

I begin by providing a more precise definition of central bank independence (CBI). \textcite{debelle_how_1994} distinguishes two core types of CBI: goal and instrument independence. Instrument independence handles the actual implementation of monetary policy to achieve those goals. When a central bank has instrument independence, is it able to utilize the tools of monetary policy to best achieve preset goals independently from political influence. In such a system, a political agent, such as the legislature or a president, is unable to influence the decision-making process for choosing which tools to use and how. For instance, if a central bank has instrument independence, a president cannot use their political influence to coerce a central bank into lowering interest rates, even if the president believes this is the best way to achieve the goals of monetary policy. If a central bank has goal independence, it has the power to choose what objectives it is attempting to achieve with its instruments. It can make judgements about what the most desirable economic outcome is, and then it can use its instrument independence to achieve these objectives. 



The Federal Reserve's institutional arrangement can help us distinguish these two forms of independence. Federal Reserve Chair Jerome \textcite{powell_panel_2023} applied Debelle and Fischer's framework to the Fed. In the 1977 amendment to the Federal Reserve Act, Congress established that the Federal Reserve has a tripartite mandate to achieve the goals of ``maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates" through its monetary policy. The Federal Reserve coloquially refers to this as the ``dual mandate" of stable prices and maximum employment, dropping the moderate long-term interest rate component because of its ``redundancy" \parencite[15]{downey_our_2025}\footnote{I will address this further in my analysis section}. Powell then argues that ``The existence of the dual mandate reflects the fact that the Federal Reserve's monetary policy independence corresponds to operational, or instrument, independence, rather than goal independence." 

%In this paper, when I refer to central bank independence (CBI), I am referring to instrument independence alone. I will explicitly distinguish goal and instrument independence when necessary as I construct my argument.


The theoretical foundation for central bank independence in the economics literature lies in a few seminal works.  \textcite{kydland_rules_1977} were the first to apply the time-inconsistency and credible commitment public policy problem to the inflation-unemployment tradeoff detailed in economics literature. In brief, in a model economy with rational agents and a government seeking to maximize some ``social objective function," governments will naturally have a temptation to implement a surprise expansionary (inflationary) policy that temporarily pushes down unemployment \parencite[473]{kydland_rules_1977}. Rational agents predict this action, so they set their inflation expectations higher, meaning that the surprise action the government would implement would trigger even higher inflation. This principle makes credible commitment by governments challenging, so the end result is heightened inflation expectations from agents. Inflation becomes anchored at a level that is higher than what the social objective function deems optimal.

\textcite{barro_rules_1983} apply this logic directly to monetary authorities. First, note that they (along with much other theoretical literature on monetary policy) assume that monetary policy is neutral in the long-run. This principally means that while the amount of currency in circulation may increase with expansionary monetary policy, the relative price between goods should, in theory, not change after a change in monetary policy \parencite[49-50]{downey_our_2025}. Given this, \textcite{barro_rules_1983} suggests that monetary policy can only reap benefits if there is a short-run surprise, before prices can readjust. However, rational agents incorporate this in their inflation expectations, pushing up inflation without reaping any rewards if monetary policy is conducted under discretion. While a central bank may seek to preserve its reputation, thereby creating some credibility regarding its policies, this is only a partial substitute. \textcite{barro_rules_1983} argue that the optimal setup is a central bank that is entirely constrained by rules, thereby establishing credibility and anchoring inflation expectations of agents. \textcite[1177]{rogoff_optimal_1985} provide several possible solutions, including appointing a ``conservative central banker" who is ``known to place a greater weight on inflation stabilization" than the general public will suggest. In particular, Rogoff's conservative central banker ``places a larger weight on inflation relative to employment stabilization" \textcite[2]{romelli_trends_2024}. They will be less tempted to implement inflationary policy, and rational agents set inflation expectations lower as a consequence. While they acknowledge that this may not be theoretical optimal, the conservative central banker argument underpins much of the empirical literature defending CBI.

Central bank independence has become the dominant form of monetary policymaking around the world. This process occurred over a period of decades following the end of World War II, but much modern analysis of CBI begins with the late 1970s/early 1980s Federal Reserve. During the stagflation episode of the late 1970s, where oil supply shocks meant high inflation occurred during a period of heightened employment, Paul Volcker assumed became Fed Chair in 1979. He chose explicitly to prioritize combatting inflation over unemployment, despite pressure from President Reagan to lower interest rates. During his tenure, the Fed raised interest rates significantly, putting the economy in a recession while simultaneously bringing down inflation. This episode is frequently referenced in the economics literature for two reasons. First, it serves as a simple empirical example of the time inconsistency problem, where a misguided politician wanted interest rates lower than what was optimal. Second, the Federal Reserve technocrats used their special expertise to successfully combat an inflation episode. Even though there was short-term pain in the economy, the US reaped the long-term benefits, providing legitimacy to the technocrats of central banks holding unique, strong monetary policy powers. Under these assumptions, CBI became the institutionally accepted arrangement for central banking around the world. ``Central bank independence has achieved an almost taken for granted quality in contemporary political life, with little questioning of its logic or effectiveness" \parencite[47]{mcnamara_rational_2002}.


\subsection{Political Literature on Central Banking}

\label{sec:political_sci_lit}

I now turn to the political science and political literature on CBI. There is a well established body of work questioning these assumptions. I focus on academic pieces whose content differs from the assumptions made in the previously discussed economics literature.  In later sections, I will apply these critiques to the economics literature, emphasizing how they are neglected, and how the economics literature, rather than the political science literature, dominates policymaking.

An initial, prerequisite point that is worth visiting is that an independent central bank can only exist and, therefore influence economic outcomes in a functioning democracy. 

Literature has critiqued the conservative central banker model for CBI.  

Some literature has questioned the narrative that the proliferation of CBI is tied to economic performance and the time inconsistency problem. \textcite{powell_panel_2023} links the expansion of CBI to poor economic performance (CHECK THIS).  In particular, it is unclear what makes monetary policy unique among economic policies. One could imagine a situation where politicians could pass irresponsible economic policy, like large tax cuts or direct payments to citizens, immediately before elections. Part of the justification that monetary policy is unique ties it to the theory of neutrality of monetary policy in the long-run. In theory, monetary policy \textit{only} has an impact on inflation, not the real economy, if sufficient time has passed. While other economic policies have real consequences far into the future, this should not. 

The assumption of monetary policy as neutral in long-run has its roots in a very simplistic understanding of moentary policy. However, monetary policy is not simple, particularly following the 2008 recession. At minimum, monetary policy is implemented \textit{through banks} exclusively. The Federal Reserve has no power to influence the money supply directly; it is banks who adjust their lending based on the supply and demand of currency. Thus, there is a double delegation of monetary policy under CBI: from the legislature to the central bank, and from the central bank to banks. This second delegation requires important decisions that have distributional consequences. At minimum, the interest rate on excess reserves explicitly changes the cost of lending reserves, even though historically, this rate was zero. Which banks can participate in lending facilities is also subject to debate- and which banks are deemed sufficiently important to the economy in the event of a crisis as well. 


Built into the time inconsistency problem, as previously described, is an assumption that politicians should not be entrusted, primarily because of their incentives to stimulate the economy before elections and because of their lack of expertise on monetary policy. This implies that the technocrats within a central bank are both unbiased and have a unique skillset that is important to guide monetary policy.  The assumption that the Fed is a technocracy has been an important justification for its own institution building. However, there is no such thing as either an unbiased bureaucrat or a universally optimal monetary policy. Economists, like all academics and policymakers, are prone to bias, from career incentives and professional socialization to personal experiences that influence how they see the world. This matters because monetary policy's implementation does not impact all citizens equally. The lens that a policymaker may view in their own life may influence their own cost-benefit analysis on monetary policy. Additionally, episodes like the 2008 recession and the post-COVID spike in inflation highlight how the Fed's technocratic justification may be imperfect. Like all agencies, it makes mistakes, but in a world of independence, it politically holds itself to a higher bar. Yet, Federal Reserve officials have demonstraterd significant shortcomings. 

Given the double delegation of policy, it seems natural that many central bankers have their roots in finance. Central banks and financial institutions work closely together, so there is an understanding of the financial system that can be beneficial. However, an overreliance on the knowledge of finance can be harmful. There is an implicit view in the conduct of monetary policy that a prosperous financial system is necessary for economic growth (and the welfare benefits growth brings to all people). Since the Volcker Federal Reserve, the growth of the financial sector has rapidly outpaced that of the rest of the US economy. Buoyed on Fed policies, the financial sector was the first to recover from both the 2008 recession and the 2020 recession. Part of the reason for this is that institutionally, the Federal Reserve needs finance; if large financial firms are unable to conduct monetary policy, then the Federal Reserve's institutional responsibilities cannot be met. Thus, there is a general lack of support for policies that require that financial firms use their institutional power to achieve social goals that are not profit maximizing. 

One political science theory that may help expalain this dynamic comes from \textcite{lindblom_market_1982}. As the role of the financial sector grew, so did the perceived costs of policies that could possibly hamper its growth. Even if that growth was suboptimal, policies protecting those not benefitting from this growth could be perceived to damage the economy. \textcite{lindblom_market_1982} likens a situation like this to a prison, where it becomes extremely difficult to justify policies that cause economic pain, even if in the long-run they create improved social welfare. The more reliant on the growth of finance the economy becomes, the more pain will be felt regarding reform. This entrenches the current monetary policy and finance relationship. The Fed can never ask too much of the financial sector, or it might risk damaging the economy and ultimately not achieving its ``dual mandate." Thus, the goals of monetary policy, and the reliance of achieving those goals on finance, are critical to understand.

There may be an endogeneity problem regarding the lack of knowledge of politicians on monetary policy, particularly in states with CBI; the disempowerment of the legislature means polticians no longer have to be educated about it. By distancing itself from the legislature, Congresspeople do not have to understand core areas of the Federal Reserve's responsibilities. There is no real requirement for them to contribute to Fed policy, and CBI provides a technical justification for them not to learn about monetary policy. It is simply outside of their control, and it is outside of their control for the good of the nation. This became apparent in 2015, when in \textcite{bernanke_courage_2017}, Chair Bernanke describes how he misconstrued the mechanism for the Federal Reserve to create reserves to establish an emergency loan to AIG, during the 2008 crisis, because he did not have the time to explain to a senator the technicalities of central banking.  


Politicians may even benefit from instituting and preserving CBI. \textcite{mcnamara_rational_2002} describes the proliferation of monetary policy as a political and institutional ideology, not a technical one. Legislatures chose to disempower themselves 

The idea that politicians are illinformed and biased while central bankers are not has helped the Federal Reserve grow its institutional responsibilities. Part of this growth has been tied to its ability to develop new, particularly expansionary policies that allow finance to grow. Policy innovation at the Fed has been central to establishing its institutional credibility as a technocracy, meaning it serves as a central form of institution building. Wansleben.

The flipside of this arrangement is that fiscal policy now becomes less and less important on influencing macroeconomic outcomes. Some central bankers describe this phenomenon as ``loneliness." Decisions with distributional or normative consequences are traditionally handled by politicians in a functioning democratic society. Voters theoretically can exercise their frustrations or support in the ballot box, ensuring that the policies implemented have their perspectives in mind\footnote{The question of whether the United States is a functioning democracy is beyond the scope of this paper. However, the scope of this paper handles whether it is good for voters to be unable to influence the goals of monetary policy.}. CBI challenges this by entrusting core macroeconomic objectives in an institution that is beyond fiscal policy. If the economy is performing poorly, Congress can point the finger and the Fed; when the economy is performing well, Congress can assert its responsibilities. This means Congress has little incentive to act on the economy; if the economy performs poorly, Congressional representatives say it is not their fault.




So far, we have established that there is a strong political science literature that questions some of the institutional arrangements of monetary policy. We also acknowledge the power that central bankers have in implementing policies, and how their individual biases and incentives may influence their policies. One important source of this bias may come from the literature they consume. \textcite{weiss_many_1979} describes an ``enlightenment" model where officials are influenced by the bulk, underlying narratives that show up in a body of academic work. Over time, research can become ``engrained" in public policy decisions, changing the way problems are framed. Given this, it is worth understanding the literature that influences policymakers at the Federal Reserve the most, and the implicit biases in that literature. This idea brings us back to where we started. Given that there is no such thing as an ``apolitical: arrangement, the norms and values that underpin an institutional, public policy setup that get defined as ``normal" and ``apolitical" are worth understanding. Literature contributes to this process by establishing core ideas as ``settled fact," pushing research and policy in a particular direction. In the case of central banking, institutional ``amnesia" occurred, and policymakers ``forget" that the arrangement they embody was different and, therefore, could be different in the future.




\section{Methodology}

In this paper, I build my arguments using a theory-testing single case study, surrounding the Federal Reserve. The Fed is among the most influential central banks around the world, given the role of the US economy and currency in foreign markets, and given how many independent central banks used the Fed as a baseline model. 

To build my arguments, I utilize the process tracing methodology developed by \textcite[1]{beach_process-tracing_2019}, defined as  ``A research method for tracing causal mechanisms using detailed, within-case empirical analysis of how a causal mechanism operated in real-world cases." Additional methodological support is provided by \textcite{bennett_process_2014}. The core idea behind process tracing to search for qualitative evidence that would exist if a given theory were true. There are a few different tests that can be used within a process tracing framework, originally developed by \textcite{van_evera_guide_1997}. I emphasize two. First, a hoop test studies a component that is necessary for a hypothesis to be true, but not sufficient to prove the truth of the hypothesis. Second, a smoking gun test strongly confirms a hypothesis if confirmed, but the failure of a such a test does not necessarily diminish the validity of the hypothesis.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Epistemological Assumptions in the Economics Literature}


\subsubsection{Theoretical Economics Modeling}

The theoretical principles for central bank independence, as previously discussed, are rooted in the time inconsistency problem from the time inconsistency problem, originally presented by \textcite{kydland_rules_1977}.  They begin their presentation of the solution with an ``agreed-upon social objective function" that is ``assumed to exist" \parencite[475]{kydland_rules_1977}. Yet, it is not always apparent in economics literature why inflation should be included in some monetary policy social objective function in the first place. \textcite[52]{downey_our_2025} notes how previous Fed Chair Ben Bernanke states, ``In the long run, the central bank can affect only
inflation, and not real variables such as output." After all, if the price for of goods increases, but buying power also increases (wages increase), consumers can purchase just as many goods as before. \textcite[58-61]{downey_our_2025} critiques the idea that there are costs to society of inflation.\footnote{In the interest of time, I generally ignore hyperinflation in this essay, but it is worth noting that there are plenty of historical examples of non-dependent central banks not experiencing hyperinflation, including the United States in the three decades following WWII.} In particular, the Federal Reserve meets the price stability component of its dual mandate by targeting 2\% inflation, measured primarily through the ``core" (excluding food and gas) Personal Consumption Expenditures index produced by the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Measuring inflation through a single measure creates problems. Different socioeconomic groups consume differently, and a single measure misses this heterogeneity. 

I emphasize two forms of heterogeneity in the impact of inflation. First, how indices define the ``relative" basket of goods used to estimate overall price increases is incredibly important. Federal Reserve officials emphasize the role that inflation can play for the poor, where families barely have enough consumption power to make ends meet, and they use this to justify their monetary policy decisionmaking. If a family is living paycheck-to-paycheck, and prices increase more than their paycheck, they may be unable to pay for essential goods and services. Meanwhile, wealthier families can use their savings or investment income the minimize the damage of inflation. Thus, it would be useful for the Federal Reserve to measure the erosion of consumption power for the poor and the influence of policy on this erosion. This, currently, is not part of the Federal Reserve's objectives.

Setting aside this heterogeneity, there is a real, measurable cost to inflation for creditors (particularly banks) relative to debtors. A core feature of bank intermediation is maturity transformation, where banks take cash (deposits) and other short-term investments and make long-term loans (mortgages, corporate loans, etc...). These loans generally have a fixed interest rate. If inflation spikes, the real value of these loans falls; each dollar a debt repays the creditor becomes worth less than when it was borrowed. Here, an aggregate measure of inflation becomes more appropriate to use. Banks distribute money into the entire economy, so the average value of a given dollar influences the ability for banks to continue lending, based on the recouped gains from previous loans. Given that there are varying costs to inflation for different interest and demographic groups, ``inflation has significant redistributive effects" \textcite[256]{posen_declarations_1995}.

It is worth noting that this story is complicated as monetary policy has obtained increased complexity. \textcite{wansleben_rise_2023} establishes how part of the Federal Reserve's growth over the last 40 years has revolved around policy innovations that make expansionary monetary policy non-harmful (or even beneficial to banks). I do not fully address this complexity in this paper, due to time. However, the simplistic models that underpin the conservative central banker also do not address these complexities. In a world of simple monetary policy, with a simple toolkit and a simple lever, there is strong evidence that financial institutions particularly benefit from tighter monetary policy that combats unexpected inflation.


Given this, it is clear that how we measure inflation, and why we care about inflation, varies significantly. Yet the theoretical literature on monetary policy begins modeling under the core premise that there is some social objective function that reflects the aggregate preferences of society. In particular, \textcite[480]{kydland_rules_1977} describe a ``socially preferred inflation rate is zero, which seems consistent with the public's preferences," and ``a more informed public might prefer some  positive or negative inflation." \textcite[103]{barro_rules_1983} acknowledge ``distortions from income taxation, unemployment compensation, and the like," yet ignores these distortions in the interior model. While, they think ``of the monetary authority’s objective as reflecting the preferences of the “representative’ private agent," they ``express this objective as a function of actual and expected rates of inflation" \parencite[102]{barro_rules_1983}. \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985} extends the modeling implications that are predicated off having one uniform, socially optimal inflation rate. The premise of the conservative central banker is a central banker who is more inflation-averse than the public. Yet, Rogoff leaves an underlying question unanswered: How might a central banker obtain this anti-inflation bias? What influences might lead to such an outcome?

One possible answer is that central bankers, as technocrats, are influenced by the economics literature and the ``facts" of monetary policy. They understand technically what monetary policy is and why it matters, far more than the public or political figures. The economics literature itself thus becomes self-reinforcing: central bankers believe the models of economics literature and believe they are optimal for society, so they follow them. However, the literature described in \ref{sec:political_sci_lit} addresses both the biases of central bankers intrinsic in policymaking and the limits of technocratic justification for the democratic delegation of monetary policymaking. 

A second possible answer is that special interest groups who uniquely benefit more from lower inflation relative to the rest of the public can help enforce central bank independence. In particular, \textcite[256]{posen_declarations_1995} argues that increasing CBI "is taken to be equivalent to increasing the relative weight of price stability over employment goals in the monetary authority's utility function," and that this "conforms with the conservative central banker of Rogoff (1985)." Posen identifies this interest group as the financial sector: ``the financial sector is uniquely positioned to provide that support, and central banks have been most independent where that support has been strongest" \parencite[254]{posen_declarations_1995}. 

%%%We can mention IOER and negative rates if we want to
The expansion of monetary policy's power, combined with the low interest rate environment since 2008 complicates the picture that finance exclusively benefits from the weighting of price stability over the employment in monetary policy goals. Large scale asset purchases are the immediate example of this, because ``by recapitalizing the financial sector and not the broader economy, QE had a political multiplier effect that enhanced the political influence of banks at the expense of ordinary citizens" \textcite[738]{adolph_missing_2018}. The core takeaway from this subsection is not that finance uniformly benefits from tight monetary policy. Instead, the development of the theoretical literature that justifies central bank independence, and thus, modern western monetary governance, is predicated on a model that explicitly overvalued a normative assumption that benefited financial institutions. Their interests were modeled as the interests of finance through the development of the ``conservative central banker," while the complexities of the distributional consequences of inflation were left out of monetary policy. In the next section, I demonstrate how the assumptions, which encoded the interests of finance, heavily influenced the empirical literature measuring the efficacy of CBI.

Economics literature has attempted to measure the influence of the aforementioned theoretical models through empirical analysis. Regarding both goal and instrument independence, it can be difficult to measure the level of influence of political figures on central bank decisions. Thus, many empirical papers distinguish between ``de jure" and ``de facto" indepndence. While de facto analysis attempts to analyze the personal influence of politics on central banking, de jure measures ground themselves in the law surrounding monetary governance, from appointment of central bank officials to the laws establishing the goals of monetary policy. I emphasize de jure independence in this paper because the assumptions in determining what an ``independent" central bank truly means are informative. These assumptions are explicitly described in the methodologies of de jure papers, but because de facto papers measure things like political influence, they have less commentary on the central banking institutionally. I describe the influence these papers have on monetary policy discourse in the next section.

Empirical measurement of de jure CBI generally involves simplifying the laws governing a central bank into an index. Authors study different components of central bank governance, and determine the degree to which the law describes an independent central bank. Indices define perfect de jure CBI as ``1" and complete de jure central bank dependence as ``0." Arguing for the merits of a given index or strategy is beyond the scope of this paper, but they all share a basic theoretical basis in the arguments initially presented by \textcite{kydland_rules_1977}, \textcite{barro_rules_1983}, and \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985}. These empirical papers generally find one result, namely that the politicization of monetary policy raises inflation without boosting economic growth \parencite[4]{posen_introduction_2025}. 

%I feel like this is an important paragraph, so we want to return to it
In the coming paragraphs, I will analyze various de jure CBI papers and argue that a core feature of this literature is the conflation of goal and operational independence. Under the \textcite{fischer_central-bank_1995} model, even though instrument independence is optimal, the goals of monetary policy should be entirely set through political processes. The type of goals a central bank is set to achieve should thus not be relevant to a CBI index if that index is principally measuring instrument independence. In particular, a codified ``price stability" target is often incorporated as part of the optimal arrangement of an independent central bank. This may be optimal for achieving the conservative central banker vision of monetary policy, but the definition itself has little to do with how independent a central bank is.

Some of the most influential works on de jure CBI are \textcite{bade_central_1988}, \textcite{alesina_credibility_1988},  \textcite{grilli_political_1991}, and \textcite{alesina_central_1993}. \textcite{alesina_credibility_1988} builds on a methodology developed by \textcite{bade_central_1988} to create one of the earliest measures of de jure independence. They decompose forms of independence into ``political" and ``financial" independence. Political independence generally deals with insulation of central bank officials from political processes (like appointment and firing protections and the government approval of monetary policy decisions) to achieve monetary policy objectives, while financial independence emphasizes if the central bank is legally obligated to finance the government. While \textcite{bade_central_1988} consider both forms of independence, \textcite{alesina_credibility_1988} focuses almost exclusively on political independence. The codified goals of monetary policy are not explicitly included in these definitions. 

This changes with \textcite[366]{grilli_political_1991}, where ``political indepencence" is defined as ``the capacity to choose the final goal of monetary policy". This is distinct from economic independence, or the ``capacity to choose the instruments with which to pursue these goals". Political independence can be mapped as a precursor to \textcite{fischer_central-bank_1995}'s goal independence, while economic independence is similar to instrument independence.

\textcite{alesina_central_1993} produces a statistical model measuring how different levels of CBI influence economic variables like output and inflation, based on an average measurement of the degree of independence described by \textcite{alesina_credibility_1988} and \textcite{grilli_political_1991}. They find that ``while central bank independence promotes price stability, it has no measurable impact on real economic performance" \textcite[151]{alesina_central_1993}. Additionally, there is ``a near perfect negative correlation between inflation and central bank independence" \textcite[153]{alesina_central_1993}. This finding has been cited thousands of times throughout the economics literature, and as we will discuss in section XXX, has made a large impact on policymakers at the Federal Reserve. Most importantly, \textcite{alesina_central_1993} accept the inclusion of ``the 'price stability' objective is explicitly and prominently part of central bank statute" in \textcite[153]{grilli_political_1991}'s work as an essential part of the definition of political independence.

At approximately the same time, \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} produced an expanded index on de jure CBI. They included more countries and more components than previous works, and as a consequence, this work has been repeatedly extended. For instance, \textcite{cukierman_central_2002} applied the original 1992 framework to an expanded sample of central banks, including those created following the breakup of the Soviet Union. More modern economics literature has built upon and modfied the indices produced by \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992}, including, but not limited to, \textcite{romelli_political_2022}, \textcite{romelli_trends_2024}, \textcite{dincer_central_2024}, and \textcite{garriga_revisiting_2025}. Alex Cukierman also extended this work in a variety of peer-reviewed work (I expand on some of these papers further in section \ref{sec:gov_speeches_analysis}. While these papers produce their own indices with unique weighting schemes, coding decisions, and larger samples, they all follow the same basic framework. In other words, \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992}'s methodology is the root of most modern measures of de jure CBI. Crucially, \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} no longer distinguished between the political and economic independence components when building its measures of de jure independence. However, they continue to include objectives component of independence as a component of the index. This decision is paramount; empirical analysis of de jure CBI essentially stopped distinguishing between goal and instrument independence in their methodologies therafter. The inclusion of a goal component in the 1992 index is also linked explicitly back to the previously described theoretical literature: ``In \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985}'s terminology, the objectives variable measures the strength of the 'conservative bias' of the bank's charter" \parencite[357]{cukierman_measuring_1992}. The conservative central banker was officially synonymous with CBI, methodologically speaking.

Recall that the United States legally has a ``tripartite" monetary policy mandate which it coloqially refers to as its ``dual mandate."\footnote{While in practice this may not significantly alter monetary policy, the ability for the Federal Reserve to simply ignore a piece of the law compromises the principle of goal independence \parencite{downey_our_2025}.} That said, all of the aforementioned de jure measures of CBI code the Federal Reserve's monetary policies as the ``dual mandate." The underlying problem for these indices is that if Congress decided to revise the Federal Reserve's mandate, this decision would alter the Federal Reserve's CBI score. For instance, if Congress eliminated the unemployment component of the dual mandate, the United States CBI score would universally increase. This intrinsic action does not influence, definitionally, goal \textit{or} instrument independence, because Congress would still be responsible for setting monetary policy objectives (goal dependence) and would not have changed its ability to influence individual policy decisions (instrument independence). However, the measurement of CBI in every de jure index would significantly changed.

The implication is clear. According to the economics literature, de jure central bank independence is not about whether a central bank can utilize its goals freely to meet the political goals established by law. Instead, central bank independence is measured as the closeness to the conservative central banker ideal that is embodied by the financial sector. Deviations from this ideal are explicitly penalized, by construction.

\subsection{Vessel of transmission: Governors' Speeches}
\label{sec:gov_speeches_analysis}
So far, we have established that the ``conservative" central banker is both a version of central banking that effectively protects the interests of the financial sector, and that definition of central bank independence in empirical literature reflects the conservative central banker, not the nuanced view of distinguishing between goal and instrument independence. I know argue that the economics literature, and its epistemological priors, has influenced the way Fed policymakers communicate about independence. I make two core arguments. First, while Fed officials routinely reference the Congressional mandate that they uphold in their monetary policy, their speeches suggest that they interpret their own mandate more closely to the presentation of independence provided by the literature, where goals are not set by politicians but rather by a single understanding of the ``conservative central banker." The implication of this is that no interest group can attempt to influence the goals of monetary policy through the political process because their is no legitimate institutional alternative to the current arrangement. Second, the financial sector is no longer considered a political interest group, but rather a vessel to transmit monetary policy. It is an essential component, so their interests are baked into the institutional arrangement. 

Papers only matter if people in positions of power implement their ideas. Thus, even though I have established that the academic economics literature conflates goal and instrument independence, this only matters if that literature influences policymakers at the Federal Reserve. Four core papers that we previously discussed (\textcite{kydland_rules_1977}, \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985}, \textcite{alesina_central_1993}, and \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992} are particularly well-cited, and they were all released before the start of my sample of speeches. Thus, I focus on these speeches in my qualitative analysis. To be more hollistic in my approach, I lump any references to ``Cukierman" together because many papers published by Alex Cukierman, like \textcite{cukierman_central_2002} and \textcite{cukierman_central_2008}, build upon or contain strong references to the 1992 methodology.



Federal Reserve governors make speeches to communicate important ideas about monetary policy and the institution. Thus, looking at the way they communicate about economic research, and what they choose to emphasize, is informative in understanding how the epistemological prior in the academic literature influences policymakers. First, Federal Reserve officials acknowledge and support the distinction between instrument and goal independence. For instance, \textcite{powell_panel_2023} directly cites \textcite{debelle_how_1994} to explicitly to invoke that "the Federal Reserve's monetary policy independence corresponds to operational, or instrument, independence, rather than goal independence." \textcite{kugler_central_2024} reiterates this, suggesting that ``the FOMC has been handed two coequal goals" by Congress, although `` the law provides the Federal Reserve with some degree of independence in defining them in detail."

Despite this ``coequal" nature of the dual mandate, Federal Reserve officials have a strong history of referencing the conservative central banker model in their monetary policy communication. Among all the speeches that reference \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985} in table \ref{tab:fed_cbi_citations}, none strongly criticize the conservative central banker model. There are general acknowledgements of the limitations of the model, the speeches generally endorse the core findings. Consider \textcite{bernanke_what_2004}. First, it is worth noting that the premise of this speech is to endorse the decisions made by Paul Volcker in the late 1970s. Bernanke invokes \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985} directly, stating that ``giving the central banker an objective function that differs from the true objectives of society may be useful" with regards to fighting inflation. He then suggests that ``these ideas, supported by a great deal of empirical work, have proven highly influential." In the same tradition, \textcite{powell_panel_2023} does the same, highlighting the importance of the conservative central banker model presented by \textcite{rogoff_optimal_1985} as well as the empirical evidence developed by \textcite{alesina_central_1993}. \textcite{evans_feds_2011} describes how after the 2008 recession, when fears of deflation and stagnation were dominant, many policymakers ``are conditioned by the work of Ken Rogoff" to fight inflationary tendencies in normal times. In other words, barring a financial crisis, the default assumption is for the officials to overvalue fears about inflation first. Perhaps this is why a ``“dual mandate rarely leads to fundamentally different policy prescriptions" than a mandate emphasizing only price stability \parencite{schnabel_navigating_2026}.

The empirical work of de jure indices has also heavily influence policymaker speeches, particularly when they justify the current institutional arrangement of the Federal Reserve. That is not to say, though, that policymakers have not pointed out the inconsistency of including goal independence in the de jure CBI measurement schemes, as we have established in section \ref{sec:empirical_ev_cbi_indices}. Governor Laurence Meyer is one of the first policymakers to explicitly reference de jure independence index. First, in \textcite{meyer_politics_2000}, he describes how, under the methodology of \textcite{grilli_political_1991} and \textcite{cukierman_measuring_1992}, when ``price stability is the exclusive objective of monetary policy," a central bank scores better, so the Fed is penalized for having a dual mandate. He explicitly prefers \textcite{bade_central_1988}'s measure because of this flaw. However, it is worth noting that \textcite{meyer_monetary_1996} interprets the Fed's dual mandate as primarily a price stability mandate, at least in the long-run under the doctrine of monetary neutrality.\footnote{As I describe in section \ref{sec:political_sci_lit}, this doctrine may not reflect modern monetary policymaking's complexities}. Thus, there is a tension between the two speeches. Economic theory would defend the construction of the indices because monetary policy only matters for inflation in the long-run. 

Additional speeches acknowledge the tension regarding the dual mandate and de jure CBI indices. This conversation centers around \textcite{cukierman_central_2008}, where Cukierman extended his 1992 work. Note that \textcite{cukierman_central_2008} uses a variety of caveats to the broader findings about the relationship between inflation and CBI, particularly regarding developing vs developed countries, and points to literature that questions the relationship between inflation and employment. Also, note that \textcite[723]{cukierman_central_2008} uses ``conservativeness and independence interchangeably" since they are extremely related and considers price stability is ``the primary responsibility" of a central bank. 
\textcite{fischer_central_2015} and \textcite{kugler_central_2024} both point out this tension, and criticize the literature for blurring the lines between instrument and goal independence. \textcite{kugler_central_2024} reference \textcite{romelli_trends_2024} to highlight this problem. Still, other Federal Reserve officials appeared to use the independence measures include the price stability objective. \textcite{mishkin_central_2008} uses this work to state, simply, that ``countries with the highest degree of central bank independence are found to have the best inflation performance." Similarly, \textcite{kohn_federal_2009} argues, based on \textcite{cukierman_central_2008}, that ``countries with more independent central banks experience lower and more stable rates of inflation with no sacrifice of jobs or income." 

Most starkly, \textcite{alesina_central_1993}, where much of the original conflation of goal and instrument independence became ingrained in the literature, is still prominantly referenced as a core piece of literature on CBI. My sample found 13 speeches that reference \textcite{alesina_central_1993}, 10 of which provide it as a simple, baseline citation before proceeding into a more complex point. The underlying point is that this paper is well accepted in the literature, and before proceeding into a more technical point, citing it is frequently done. It checks an academic box. Only, three speeches provide any substantive conversation on the literature beyond a simple citation. Then governor \textcite{powell_audit_2015} referred to the paper as ``seminal," although he describes how ``more recent research finds the relationship to be less solid... in part because independent central banks have in several cases been instituted precisely because economic performance has been subpar." \textcite{kugler_central_2024} acknowledges the finding in \textcite{alesina_central_1993} before arguing about the merits of maintaining an employment objective. Finally, Chair \textcite{powell_panel_2023} provides a reference to \textcite{alesina_central_1993} as ``empirical evidence" of the inflation merits of CBI (he later addresses the importance of maintaining instrument, not goal, independence).


The underlying theme regarding the economic theory is that policymakers use these papers as starting points. There core findings are rarely contested, along with their normative assumptions. Instead, they are referred to as the baseline for further conversation. Consider \textcite{mishkin_central_2008}, who describes how ``special interests may try to apply pressure on the central bank to boost employment in the short term through an overly expansionary monetary policy." Mishkin validates these points through references to \textcite{alesina_central_1993}, \textcite{kydland_rules_1977}, and various works by Cukierman. Which special interests are encoded to the models in the first place is never touched. 



\section{Conclusion}




%%%%DO NOT LIKE EXECUTIVE POWER
The mandates of monetary policy matter. Modern monetary policy is no longer a simple matter of setting interest rates and tweaking money supply. The assumption of the neutrality of monetary policy no longer is satisfactory. Quantitative easing, lending facilities, forward guidance, and the regulatory infrastructure surrounding the Fed all play an important role in the development of the economy. Given this, the normative goals of how we use these complex instruments should be the subject of public debate. Monetary policy is about more than just inflation and employment. Gone are the days when monetary policy is limited, and the Fed's toolkit can achieve a variety of different objectives. It effectively chooses the winners and losers in an economy. The winners of CBI tend to be finance, and the losers tend to be those at the bottom of the income and wealth distribution. 

In an academic context, allowing for free-flowing political, normative debate surrounding the goals of monetary policy is essential, both for the credibility of the Fed and the creation of a better macroeconomic policies. Importantly, stifling this debate under the guise of ``preserving central bank independence" is, in my view, particularly dangerous. There are so many valuable lessons in the historic literature around instrument independence, but the dangers of normative loading and the neglect of political subjects in economics literature is damaging. It means that the interests who are most strongly encoded in the baseline literature have an academic justification for continuing to win. 

\textcite{powell_panel_2023} argues that the Fed ``should "stick to our knitting" and not wander off to pursue perceived social benefits that are not tightly linked to our statutory goals and authorities." Under the historical distinction of instrument and goal independence, that makes institutional sense. Sticking to the explicit goals of monetary policy, as laid out by law, meets the standard of instrument independence. However, if the literature that underpins many of the academic arguments justifying this institutional arrangement conflate the two forms of independence, then it becomes impossible to politically challenge and better the aforementioned statutory goals. That is a political job; it is impossible to accomplish today. 

None of this matters without a functioning democracy. The literature is clear that overpowered executive can damage the credibility of a central bank by encouraging monetary policy that pays off its debts or overstimulates the economy, creating hyperinflation. This piece is not a commentary on whether the United States is a functioning democracy; democracy as a government concepts is something that needs to be preserved and protected, always. The question I ask is, in a democracy, should the public have a say in what an independent central bank seeks to accomplish. To that, I answer, unequivocally yes, and economics literature going forward should not diminish this.
